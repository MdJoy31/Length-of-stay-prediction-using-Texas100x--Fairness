\begin{frame}{Where clinical ML stands today}
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{4pt}
\item Clinical ML supports triage, risk scoring, and length-of-stay prediction across many sites~[1, 2].
\item Documented post-release subgroup failures across radiology, sepsis, kidney function, and care-allocation models~[3, 4, 5, 6].
\item Regulatory expectations have shifted toward continuous lifecycle monitoring~[7, 8] with reporting standards~[9, 10, 11, 12].
\item Auditing toolkits in routine use return a thresholded pass-or-fail verdict on a fixed snapshot~[13, 14, 15, 16].
\item That verdict is the object hospitals act on. The snapshot tells us nothing about its stability over time.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The remaining gap}
\begin{alertblock}{Real Texas-100X anchor finding (925\,128 records) [17]}
The worst-group true-positive-rate verdict for race reads ``fair'' at \textbf{0.824}. The same verdict flips to ``unfair'' in \textbf{4 of 20} hospital-network resamples (Verdict Flip Rate \textbf{0.20}). A batch audit cannot tell when, or whether, that verdict reversal recurs as new patients arrive.
\end{alertblock}
\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Four gaps follow:}
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
\item \textcolor{teal}{\textbf{Gap 1, time axis.}} Batch verdicts give no timestamp~[13, 14, 15].
\item \textcolor{teal}{\textbf{Gap 2, peeking penalty.}} Repeated inspection inflates false-alarm rates without a sequential guarantee~[18, 19, 20].
\item \textcolor{teal}{\textbf{Gap 3, diagnostic interpretation.}} A detected gap with no triage does not tell a team whether to fix data or collect more samples~[21, 22].
\item \textcolor{teal}{\textbf{Gap 4, regression output.}} Length-of-stay outputs lack a flip-rate extension~[23].
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How each planned module closes one identified gap}
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{6pt}
\item \textcolor{amber}{\textbf{Gap 1, time axis}} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Flip Hazard} (per-arrival survival curve instead of a single scalar~[24]).
\item \textcolor{amber}{\textbf{Gap 2, peeking penalty}} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Per-cell anytime-valid e-process} (Type-I control at every inspection step~[18, 19, 20]).
\item \textcolor{amber}{\textbf{Gap 3, diagnostic interpretation}} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Aleatoric/epistemic decomposition} (triage label per alarmed cell~[21]).
\item \textcolor{amber}{\textbf{Gap 4, regression output}} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{RCAP} (counterfactual rank shift for LOS regression~[23]).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% --------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Method discipline: what we will \emph{not} claim}
\small
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{4pt}
\item \textcolor{teal}{\textbf{Per-cell anytime-valid Type-I control:}} YES, under Ville inequality~[18, 19].
\item \textcolor{teal}{\textbf{Step-wise Benjamini-Hochberg across cells:}} applied at inspection time only. NOT a time-uniform online FDR guarantee~[25, 26].
\item \textcolor{teal}{\textbf{Diagnostic attribution:}} aleatoric/epistemic triage signal. NOT a confirmed causal root cause~[21].
\item \textcolor{teal}{\textbf{Counterfactual definition:}} feature-shift, computable at inference. Weaker than SCM-based counterfactual fairness~[27, 28]; may under-flag indirect effects.
\item \textcolor{teal}{\textbf{Clinical deployment:}} controlled replay and simulation only. NOT prospective EHR deployment.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
